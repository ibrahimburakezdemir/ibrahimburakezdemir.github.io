From oscilloscope to App Store: A short history of game development

A seventy-year story that runs from a bouncing dot at a lab’s open house to billions of phones. Who made the first game, and how did PC and mobile game development begin?

2 November 2026 · 10 min read

October 1958, New York. Brookhaven National Laboratory opens its doors to the public, as it does once a year. Most rooms hold photographs and instruments behind glass, and visitors are yawning. In one corner, though, a line keeps growing.

One by one, people pick up two small control boxes in front of a round oscilloscope screen. On it is a tennis court seen from the side. The goal: get a dot of light over the net without hitting it.

Physicist William Higinbotham had designed the game in a few hours and built it in three weeks. On visitors’ day, hundreds of people lined up to play it. It was called Tennis for Two, and today it counts among the earliest ancestors of an industry worth hundreds of billions of dollars.1

This article follows the road from that line to the phones in our pockets. Along the way, the same scene plays out again and again:

Whoever gets the game to the player holds the power. And every time, developers find a new way around that gate.

First sparks: games born in labs

There’s no single answer to “what was the first video game?” It depends on what you call a game.

Year Who What they made
1947 Thomas T. Goldsmith Jr. and Estle Ray Mann Filed a patent for a “cathode-ray tube amusement device” that simulated firing at targets on a screen. No computer involved; it was fully analog.2
1952 A. S. Douglas Wrote OXO, noughts and crosses on Cambridge’s EDSAC computer. The point wasn’t fun but showing human–computer interaction for his PhD thesis.3
1958 William Higinbotham Built Tennis for Two, one of the first games made purely to entertain.
1962 Steve Russell and friends at MIT Wrote Spacewar! on the PDP-1: two spaceships, a star in the middle and its gravity.4

Spacewar! was never sold. Its code was copied from lab to lab, and every team added something. What we now call a developer community first gathered around that shared code.

The first person who thought of selling games

None of these people thought about selling a game. That idea came from a television engineer, Ralph Baer.

The idea of playing games on a TV came back to Baer in August 1966, while he was waiting for a bus. The next morning he wrote a four-page proposal.5 The “Brown Box” prototype he built at Sanders Associates went on sale in 1972 as the Magnavox Odyssey, the world’s first home video game console.

The Odyssey had no sound, and the screen showed just a few white squares. The playing field came from colored plastic overlays that clung to the TV screen with static. Still, something had started: games were now at home.

Coins and an overflowing cash box: the Atari era

Games first made money in bars and arcades. Inspired by Spacewar!, Nolan Bushnell and Ted Dabney built the first commercial arcade game, Computer Space, in 1971. Players found its controls and mechanics confusing, and it fell short of the success its makers hoped for.

In 1972 the pair founded Atari and gave engineer Al Alcorn a simple tennis game as a training exercise. The first Pong machine went into a bar called Andy Capp’s Tavern and soon started acting up. The cause: a coin box stuffed to overflowing with quarters.6

Then Japan stepped in. Taito’s Space Invaders (1978) and Namco’s Pac-Man (1980) became symbols of the arcade age.7 The Atari 2600, released in 1977, brought cartridge consoles into homes.

The programmers with no names

Atari sold the games but never credited the people who wrote them.

Programmer Warren Robinett answered quietly. He hid a secret room in Adventure and put his own name inside. After the game shipped, a 15-year-old player found it. Atari left the room in, because remaking the cartridge would have cost $10,000. That’s how the tradition we now call the “easter egg” began.8

In 1979 four Atari programmers, David Crane, Alan Miller, Bob Whitehead and Larry Kaplan, left to found Activision. It became the first independent studio to make games for someone else’s console, and every instruction manual gave at least a page to the developer who made the game.9

The crash, and cartridges in a landfill

Once that door opened, hundreds of rushed games flooded the market. In 1983 the North American video game market crashed.

Its symbol became E.T., a game squeezed into five weeks of development. Unsold Atari cartridges were secretly buried in a landfill in Alamogordo, New Mexico. For years it sounded like an urban legend, until an excavation in April 2014 dug E.T. cartridges out of the ground.10

Nintendo put the industry back on its feet. It launched the Famicom in Japan in 1983. When it brought the NES to America, starting in New York in 1985, it called it an “Entertainment System” and, to win a place on store shelves, packed it with a little robot called R.O.B. Studios that wanted to make games for it needed a license, and approved games got the “Seal of Quality”.11 The console maker standing at the gate and vetting developers is a model that still holds today.

Programmers at home: the rise of PC games

While console makers held the gate, anyone could write and sell a game for a personal computer. The industry’s boldest ideas came out of that openness.

University computers

Before home computers, games lived on universities’ giant machines. The University of Illinois’ PLATO network saw some of the first multiplayer games and dungeon crawlers.12

Around the same time, programmer and caver Will Crowther wrote Colossal Cave Adventure, based on his trips and maps of Mammoth Cave in Kentucky. Don Woods expanded it in 1977. The team behind MIT’s Zork later founded Infocom.13

Zip-lock bags and cassettes

In 1977 the Apple II, Commodore PET and TRS-80 went on sale. You no longer needed a university to write a game.14

  • Richard Garriott wrote Akalabeth in high school and sold it in zip-lock bags for $20 each. Next came Ultima, one of the founding series of the RPG genre.15
  • Ken and Roberta Williams founded Sierra On-Line with Mystery House (1980), designed by Roberta. It was one of the first adventure games with graphics.16
  • Electronic Arts, founded in 1982, called its developers “software artists”. It sold games in square boxes that looked like album covers and featured its developers in magazine ads like musicians.17

In the UK, the ZX Spectrum and Commodore 64 gave rise to a generation of bedroom coders. Teenagers recorded the games they wrote onto cassettes and sold them by mail. Elite, written for the BBC Micro in 1984 by Cambridge students David Braben and Ian Bell, became the legend of that era.18

Shareware and Doom

In the 1990s developers found a way to reach players without a publisher. Scott Miller at Apogee popularized shareware: give away the first episode, sell the rest.19

id Software (John Carmack and John Romero) used it for Wolfenstein 3D (1992) and then Doom (1993). Doom’s first episode went up on the University of Wisconsin’s file server at midnight on December 10, 1993. Thousands of people trying to download it at once brought down the university’s network.20

Doom changed the industry in three ways:

  1. Shareware put it on millions of computers.
  2. Its WAD files let players build their own levels, which started modding culture.
  3. It established engine licensing. id’s later Quake engine became the foundation of Valve’s Half-Life.

Today’s toolbox

Most of the tools we use to make PC games today took shape between 1995 and 2010:

  • Windows 95 and DirectX (1995): Microsoft moved games from DOS to Windows.21
  • 3D graphics cards: the 3dfx Voodoo (1996) and NVIDIA GeForce 256 (1999) made 3D acceleration standard.
  • Off-the-shelf engines: Epic’s Unreal Engine (1998) and Unity (2005) meant you no longer had to write an engine from scratch.22
  • Digital distribution: Valve’s Steam (2003) took games out of the box and onto the internet.23

Put together, these tools led to the indie boom after 2008. Braid, World of Goo and Minecraft (2009), started by a single developer, showed that small teams could compete with giant studios.24

An arcade in your pocket: the birth of mobile

The power of a snake

One of the first games on a phone was Tetris on the Hagenuk MT-2000 in 1994. What put mobile games into millions of pockets was Snake on the Nokia 6110, announced at the end of 1997.25

Nokia engineer Taneli Armanto had actually wanted to put a Tetris-like game on the phone. When rights issues got in the way, he picked the snake. That simple game became a generation’s first mobile game.

In the early 2000s, J2ME (Java) and Qualcomm’s BREW let phones download games.26 They were sold through carrier portals. Publishing a game meant striking a separate deal with each carrier, and the carrier kept most of the money.

The App Store gold rush

On July 10, 2008, Apple opened the App Store with about 500 apps. For the first time, a solo developer could sell a game worldwide without negotiating with carriers.27

That opportunity soon turned into real stories. Ethan Nicholas, who worked at Sun Microsystems, wrote an artillery game called iShoot in his spare time. Within weeks it earned him about $600,000, and he quit his job.28 Stories like his pulled thousands of developers onto the platform. In October 2008 Google opened Android Market, the forerunner of today’s Google Play.

The story of the era’s biggest hit is a lesson of its own. Before Finland’s Rovio released Angry Birds in December 2009, it had made fifty-one other games, most of them for other publishers. Success came on attempt fifty-two.29

The price of free

What really changed the business of mobile games was Apple allowing in-app purchases in free apps in October 2009.30 Social games on Facebook, especially Zynga’s FarmVille, had already rehearsed that model.

In 2012 Supercell’s Clash of Clans and King’s Candy Crush Saga arrived on phones. “Download for free, pay inside” became the industry standard. Today mobile games make up more than half of global games revenue: according to Newzoo, $113.3 billion of a $201.6 billion market in 2025.31

A note from Türkiye

In this latest wave, Türkiye became one of the world’s most visible mobile game hubs.

  • Peak Games was sold to Zynga in 2020 for about $1.8 billion, one of the largest exits in Turkish tech history.32
  • Gram Games joined Zynga in 2018, and Rollic in 2020.33
  • Dream Games, founded in 2019 by former Peak employees, scored a worldwide hit with Royal Match in 2021.34
  • On PC, Ankara-based TaleWorlds built a worldwide fan base with Mount & Blade (2008) and its sequels.35

The gates changed, the story didn’t

You can read seventy years of game history as a list of gatekeepers and the people who got around them:

Era Who held the gate The way developers found
1970s Atari: no credit, no royalties for programmers Activision: the first studio to make games for someone else’s console
1980s Console makers and their licensing Home computers: anyone can write and sell a game
1990s Store shelves and publishers Shareware: first episode free, the rest for sale
2000s Boxed PC games Steam and indie games
2008 Mobile carriers App Store and Google Play: selling worldwide with no middleman

Today the gate is held by the App Store, Google Play and Steam, and their fees and rules are among the most debated topics in the industry.

If history teaches anything, the next way around will probably start in a lab or a bedroom, as somebody’s side project. At The Happy Smash Iron, we’re one of the newest links in this story: Flicklane is standing right at that gate, getting ready for the App Store and Google Play.
